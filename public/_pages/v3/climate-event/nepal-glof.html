What is a GLOF

A flood with no rain in it.

Picture a bucket made of ice and loose rubble, parked high on a mountain. The glacier left it there when it melted back, and it has been filling with meltwater ever since. Nobody built it. Nothing holds it but the rubble.

Now global heating — heating, not warming — thins the ice binding that rubble together. The walls were never strong. They are getting weaker.

Then something falls in. A slab of rock. A wall of ice. The bucket empties down the valley all at once, and it does it in minutes.

By the time anyone downstream knows, it is already there.

The technical version

GLOF — Glacial lake outburst flood.

The sudden release of meltwater ponded behind a moraine or ice dam. The dam fails when it is overtopped, undercut or destabilised — usually by a rock or ice avalanche into the lake, or by thawing permafrost loosening the frozen debris that holds it together. Failure lasts minutes, and discharge can run orders of magnitude above the river's normal flow.

Not a cloudburst. Not a monsoon flood. A GLOF can happen on a clear day.

The danger is not a distant hypothetical. The question is whether monitoring, planning and construction follow what is already known.

And they are not separate hazards. This is one chain.

  1. Warming and glacier retreat
  2. Unstable ice, rock and slopes; glacial lakes forming and expanding
  3. A trigger — extreme rain, avalanche, landslide or earthquake
  4. River blockage, or a lake outburst
  5. Debris flow and flash flood
  6. Roads, bridges, hydropower and towns in the path
  7. Mass casualties, tens of kilometres downstream

Flood, landslide, cloudburst, avalanche, outburst — described one at a time, they look like separate accidents. They are a sequence, and it is why assessment project by project cannot see what arrives catchment by catchment.

What science can say

Climate change does not pull every trigger, but it is loading the system: glaciers retreat, new lakes form and existing ones expand, moraine dams and frozen rock lose their binding, and slopes become less stable. Across the Third Pole, outburst risk is projected to roughly triple by 2100 — with ICIMOD's own caveat that a higher hazard has not yet produced a higher rate of floods.

What has not been attributed

Nobody has established that climate change caused THIS event. Single-event attribution takes months, and for a glacier or rock collapse it is often not possible at all. A rising hazard across a region is not a finding about one flood in it.

All 16 standing figures for this hazard, and their sources

A glacial lake outburst is a flood with no rain in it. Meltwater collects behind a moraine or an ice dam, something displaces it — a rockfall, an avalanche, a thaw — and the dam fails in minutes. The Himalaya holds tens of thousands of these lakes, India monitors a fraction of them, and the warning systems are almost entirely unbuilt.

How would we know?
Glacial lake breach
The lake, before and after. An emptied lake shows at 10 m, and the area change is measurable — usually settled within days of the first clear pass.
Ice or rock avalanche
A detachment scar on the slope above, and seismometers, which time a rock-ice collapse to the second. This is how Chamoli 2021 was reclassified.
Extreme rainfall
Gauge or radar rainfall in the hours before. A true outburst has no rain in it, so a heavy-rain record is evidence AGAINST one.
Landslide blockage and collapse
A temporary lake upstream, and a discharge record that rises, falls, then rises higher. Often established only after the second flood.
Thawing permafrost
Ground temperature and modelling of the slope. A condition rather than a trigger — it was the finding at Sikkim in 2023.

The mechanism in general

Trigger
A rockfall or avalanche into the lake, a surge over the moraine, or permafrost loosening the debris behind it. Minutes long, and no rain in it.
Conditions
Retreating glaciers leave meltwater ponded behind loose moraine, never engineered. Himalayan mass loss rose 65% from the 2000s to the 2010s, so the lakes are more numerous, larger and younger.
What science says
Outburst risk across the Third Pole roughly triples by 2100, high or very high risk lakes going from 1,203 to 2,963. ICIMOD's caveat: higher hazard has not yet produced more floods.
Human factors
What was built in the path decides the disaster. Sikkim 2023 destroyed a 1,200 MW dam and about 25,900 buildings on the Teesta. Nepal's Bhote Koshi warning gives roughly six minutes, its sensors all below the border the water starts above.

The mechanism for this kind of event, not a finding about this one. Himalayan disasters are often reclassified once fieldwork is done.

What happened in each, and what was learned
South Lhonak Lake, Sikkim · 3–4 October 2023
South Lhonak Lake, Sikkim. 14.7 million m³ of frozen moraine collapsed into the lake, raising a wave around 20 m high that breached the dam and released some 50 million m³ of water. It destroyed the 1,200 MW Teesta-III dam at Chungthang and travelled 385 km down the Teesta into Bangladesh, damaging about 25,900 buildings and 31 major bridges. There was no functioning early warning system; what warning reached people came informally from border police upstream.
Chorabari Lake, above Kedarnath · 16–17 June 2013
Chorabari Lake above Kedarnath, Uttarakhand, overtopped its moraine and drained in five to ten minutes — the second phase of the disaster, after a rainfall-driven debris flow the day before. A genuine lake outburst, though the lake was rain-fed rather than glacier-fed.
Dig Tsho, Nepal · 4 August 1985
Dig Tsho, Nepal. An ice avalanche into the lake overtopped and cut the end moraine; six to ten million m³ drained in about four hours, destroying a nearly-complete hydro plant 11 km downstream and 14 bridges. It struck during a lull in the trekking season.
Chamoli, Uttarakhand — and not a GLOF · 7 February 2021
Chamoli, Uttarakhand — commonly filed as a glacial lake outburst and not one. About 27 million m³ of rock and glacier ice fell from Ronti Peak and became a debris flow. No lake was involved; the peer-reviewed account was written specifically to correct the early reporting.

What these counts leave out

  • Lakes below the monitoring threshold. CWC monitors above 10 hectares; the July 2025 Rasuwa flood came from a supraglacial lake in Tibet that had not been noticed at all.
  • Anything upstream of the border. India has no gauge above the Tibetan frontier on the Brahmaputra system, and Nepal's Bhote Koshi warning system has a lead time of about six minutes because its sensors are all on the Nepali side of a catchment that begins in Tibet.
  • The distinction between hazard types, in early reporting. Rock-ice avalanches, landslide-dam breaks and true lake outbursts are routinely reported as one another in the first days, and the correction usually arrives months later in a journal.
