How the number is made

One number stands in for eight, and it is not their average — it is the worst of them. Measured and modelled are set differently, on purpose.

AQI

One number for eight poisons

Eight pollutants folded into one 0–500 figure that reports whichever is worst at a monitor, then averaged across Delhi’s monitors. It is an index, not a concentration. 140 today; the limit is 100.

PM2.5

Small enough to enter blood

Particles under two and a half microns pass the lung wall into the bloodstream. About 159.7 µg/m³ at Wazirpur today, implied by its sub-index. The Indian daily standard is 60.

PM10

Dust you can feel

Coarser particles from roads, construction and soil, stopping in the upper airway. About 159.7 µg/m³ there today. The standard is 100.

The big numbers are CPCB’s own sub-indexes, published as such; the small ones under them are the concentrations those sub-indexes imply, carrying a tilde because this feed publishes no concentration at all. Until 25 August 2026 this page read the sub-indexes as µg/m³ and converted them again, which roughly doubled every figure on it.

AQI 100 is not a rule of thumb. The boundary sits at PM2.5 60 µg/m³ and PM10 100 µg/m³ — exactly the 24-hour standards India set for itself. Above 100 is above the law.

Where does it come from?

A source apportionment is a model: it takes measured concentrations, wind and an emissions inventory and works backwards to a plausible split. Biomass burning is one segment, and it has a season.

Modelled, not measured

This is the only complete split anyone has published for Delhi, and its newest measurement is from February 2017. Six sectors, summing to a hundred, from the study the Government of India commissioned to settle the question.

WinterSummerΔ
Industry 30% 22% -8
Transport 28% 17% -11
Dust 17% 38% +21
Others 11% 8% -3
Residential 10% 8% -2
Agricultural burning 4% 7% +3

Dust more than doubles in summer and industry stays put. The season does not just change how much there is — it changes what it is. A control measure aimed at the winter split is aimed at a different problem in June.

TERI-ARAI, August 2018 — PM2.5, Delhi, dispersion model (WRF–CMAQ), monitoring April 2016 to February 2017. Commissioned by the Department of Heavy Industry, Ministry of Heavy Industries and Public Enterprises. The report, Table E.2, page E13, executive summary.

Four things the study says about itself. Agricultural burning at 4% is a floor, not an estimate — the monitoring never covered October, when burning peaks, and the report says so in as many words. The shares are averaged across the whole period, so they cannot describe a bad day. The model reproduces only 82–87% of the mass actually measured. And “industry” includes biomass burned as industrial fuel, which the report itself calls an overestimate.

Modelled, not measured

The other government study will not give you a single number. IIT Kanpur sampled six sites across Delhi and reported every source as a range, because the answer was different under every monitor. The width of each line below is how much the two studies, and the six sites, fail to agree.

Vehicles are somewhere between 6% and 29%. That is the range in which every argument about Delhi’s cars, buses and trucks is actually being conducted. TERI-ARAI puts transport at 17–28% and says plainly that its figure is higher than IIT Kanpur’s because it counted secondary particles alongside primary ones — the two studies are not quite measuring the same thing.

IIT Kanpur, January 2016 — PM2.5, receptor modelling (chemical mass balance) at six Delhi sites, monitoring Winter 2013-14 and summer 2014. Commissioned by the Government of NCT of Delhi, and the Delhi Pollution Control Committee. The report, Section 4.6, 'Overall Summary and Source Apportionment at a Glance', page 241.

And the categories do not line up. IIT Kanpur’s “coal and flyash” is a fuel signature; TERI-ARAI’s “industry” is a sector that bundles power plants, brick kilns and stone crushers. They are not two estimates of one quantity. They do not average, and neither is a measurement of today’s reading.

Modelled, not measured

Inside the 28% that transport contributes, cars are 3.4. Trucks are more than twice that and two-wheelers twice that. Winter PM2.5, Delhi.

Trucks 8%
Two-wheelers 7%
Three-wheelers 5%
Cars 3.4%

Of the car share, diesel is 67–74% and the oldest cars dominate — BS-II and earlier account for 31–50% of it. Run the study’s own arithmetic and every BS-IV diesel car in Delhi comes to about 0.5–0.9% of PM2.5. That is not an argument for more cars. It is an argument about where a policy rupee buys the most air, and the answer in this table is trucks, brick kilns and generators, not hatchbacks.

Also inside the totals: within industry’s 30%, brick kilns 8%, power stations 6%, stone crushers 2%, and 14% from other industries burning coal, biomass, pet-coke and furnace oil. Within “others”, diesel generators 5% and refuse burning 3%. And within dust’s 17%, road dust is 4 and construction 1 — the report attributes much of the remainder to dust that did not originate in Delhi at all. TERI-ARAI, section E7.3.

The question neither study answers. What is in the air today. Both are seasonal averages whose last measurement is eight years old. There is a system that does answer it — Decision Support System (DSS), run by the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology, Pune, which publishes a daily split across 29 sectors with the stubble share taken from the previous evening’s satellite fire counts. It has no public API and its host was unreachable from the machine that built this page, so it is named and linked and never restated.

What has been spent on it

The cost of inaction is more than the action

Allocated is the claim. Utilised is the record. Both are dwarfed by the damage — and all three numbers are the government’s own.

The damage, each year

₹7 lakh crore

about $95 billion, roughly 3% of GDP — the report’s own comparisons are 50% of all tax collected annually, and 150% of India’s health budget. Dalberg with Clean Air Fund and CII, 2021.

Released for it since 2019

₹13,415 crore

NCAP and the 15th Finance Commission air-quality grants, cumulative.

Actually spent

₹9,929 crore

74% of what was released. The 82 NCAP cities used ₹831 crore of ₹1,615 crore — 51%.

One year of damage costs about fifty times everything released for it since 2019, and about seventy times what has been spent. Both periods are stated because the comparison only holds if they are: the damage figure is annual, the spending figures are cumulative.

What this is not. It is not a cost-benefit study — nobody has published a costed abatement plan for Delhi-NCR, so this page cannot tell you what fixing the air would cost. It is the narrower claim, and the only one the figures support: what the damage costs each year, beside what has actually been released and spent against it. Sources: PIB releases, CPCB’s PRANA funding guidelines, CREA’s Tracing the Hazy Air, Dalberg with Clean Air Fund and CII. No inference is drawn beyond the arithmetic.

Build a monitor, and then distrust it

A classroom-grade PM sensor costs a few thousand rupees — an optical particle counter, a microcontroller, a power supply. It will give you a PM2.5 number within minutes of switching on. That number means nothing yet.

Low-cost optical sensors read high in humidity and drift as their chamber fouls. To turn a reading into a measurement you co-locate it beside a reference station for a fortnight and fit your device against theirs. That is the whole difference between the 43 instruments on this page and a gadget on a windowsill, and it is why this page will not accept a crowd-sourced reading as equivalent.

File a complaint that lands somewhere

Open burning, a construction site without sheeting, a generator running in a residential block, a road left unswept — each is already prohibited under rules somebody is meant to enforce.

Sameer, CPCB’s own app, publishes the same station data this page reads and takes a geotagged photograph as a complaint. Green Delhi, the Delhi government’s app, routes the same thing to the responsible department with a trackable number. Neither is Swechha’s and neither is endorsed — they are named because a complaint with a reference number is harder to lose than a complaint without one.

Read the objection window

Environmental clearances carry a public-consultation stage with a fixed window for written objections. Most close unopposed because nobody was watching.

This page does not yet track those windows for Delhi-NCR. It is named here because it is the highest-leverage thing on the list, not because it is built.

Every reading, kept

Each reading keeps its own address, with the source that produced it, when it was observed, and the limit it was judged against. Nothing is overwritten when it improves and nothing is quietly restated when it gets worse. An empty day stays empty — a gap in the record is a gap in the record, never a zero.

And why it is not a reply

None of it moves an AQI of 307. A campaign changes what a city is willing to say about its air; it does not change what the city burns. The reading at the top of this page is the honest measure of that distance, which is why it is printed before any of this.
