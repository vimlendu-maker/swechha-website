What that means, in one line

A fish needs about four milligrams of oxygen in every litre of water to stay alive. The law asks for five. At 5 places in Delhi the instrument cannot find nought point three — and nought point three is not a measurement, it is the smallest number the method is able to print.

The government’s own word for it is BDL: below detection limit. Not low. Not measurable at all.

The river arrives alive and leaves dead, and it happens inside one city. At Palla, where the Yamuna enters Delhi, oxygen reads 5.4–9.8 mg/L. 6 stations later, at Okhla okhla, after the shahdara drain, it reads 0.3. The distance between those two readings is the length of Delhi.

Who is in it

A dead river is not an aesthetic problem. It is a sanitation one, and it is the city’s own sewage that killed it.

3,596

million litres of sewage a day, Delhi

Delhi Jal Board’s own estimate, given to Parliament in July 2025. The city produces this every day, and it has to go somewhere.

1,582

million litres a day that reach the river untreated, or treated below the standard

44% of everything the city produces. 3596 generated - 2014 compliant. Both numbers are in the same paragraph of the same reply.

1.6 crore

faecal bacteria per 100 millilitres, at Okhla okhla, after the shahdara drain

A hundred millilitres is about a third of a glass. The legal limit is 2,500. This is 6,400 times it, and it is measured immediately after two of Delhi’s drains join the river.

Counted or measuredModelled

Read the last number again. It is not a pollution index or a score. It is a count of bacteria that come out of human bodies, in water that people stand in, wash in, immerse idols in and hold ceremonies beside — every week, at ghats along this stretch.

How the number is made

How the number is made

Four measurements, four published limits, and one of them is not really a measurement at all.

The Yamuna is judged on four things. All four limits come from the Primary Water Quality Criteria notified under the Environment (Protection) Rules, 1986 — and all four are printed in the header of the very table the readings come from. The measurement and the limit are on the same page of the same document. Nothing here had to be looked up, inferred or chosen.

Dissolved oxygen · legal minimum > 5.0 mg/L

Oxygen dissolved in the water, in milligrams per litre. It is the one measurement where a high number is good, and it is the closest thing to a pulse a river has. Below about 4 mg/L most fish cannot survive. Below 2, almost nothing can.

This is the number that reads 0.3. And 0.3 is the reporting floor of the method — the smallest value it can print. CPCB writes it as 0.3(BDL): below detection limit. The honest reading is “at or below 0.3”, and the honest word is not low but absent. This page never renders it as 0.0, because 0.0 would be a claim the method cannot support.

Biochemical oxygen demand · legal maximum < 3.0 mg/L

BOD measures how much oxygen the bacteria in the water would consume while eating what is dissolved in it. In plain terms: how much rot is in the river. A high BOD and a low oxygen are the same event described from two ends.

The worst Delhi reading is 72.0 mg/L at Okhla okhla, after the shahdara drain — 24 times the limit of 3.0. 7 of the 7 Delhi stations are over it.

Faecal coliform · legal maximum < 2500 MPN/100 mL

Bacteria that live in the guts of warm-blooded animals, counted per 100 millilitres. They are measured not because they are all dangerous but because their presence means sewage, and sewage carries what does the harm.

The count is expressed as MPN — most probable number — because the method is statistical rather than a headcount. 6 of 7 Delhi stations are over the limit, the worst by 6,400 times.

pH · legal range 6.5 – 8.5

How acidic or alkaline the water is. It is the one parameter on this page that Delhi mostly passes, and it is included for exactly that reason: a page that only shows the failures is not an instrument, it is an argument.

Every Delhi station in the 2025 table sits inside 6.5 – 8.5 or within a tenth of it. pH tells you almost nothing about whether a river is alive, which is why it is never the headline.

Source. National Water Quality Monitoring Programme — water quality data of river Yamuna, 2025 — the document, 33 stations from Yamunotri to Prayagraj, 7 of them in Delhi. 16 of the 21 monthly oxygen readings taken in Delhi in the first half of 2025 were below the detection limit. Not falling. Not seasonal. The same reading, every month, at every station downstream of the city.

Blank cells are blank in the source. March oxygen is missing at two stations and March bacteria at one, and they are left empty rather than carried forward from February or interpolated.

There is no machine-readable multi-year series for this river. CPCB republishes the same measurements in a different column layout every year — the 2023 file is an all-rivers document with extra columns where the 2025 one has pH — so a parser written for one year silently reads the wrong column for another.

So instead of a series, one check that survives any layout: the value against its own station name.

Two years apart, three stations, the same number — and that number is the floor of the method. Whatever has been done between those two readings has not moved this one.

This is deliberately not drawn as a trend line. Two points are not a trend, and a line between them would imply a rate this data cannot support.

How much the public looks up the Yamuna, month by month, measured as views of the English Wikipedia article. It measures attention, never water quality.

Peak 1,07,165 in Jul 2023 against a floor of 9,118 in Mar 2026 — a swing of 11.8×. The peak is the month the river flooded Delhi. The oxygen reading did not change in that month, or in any other.

A quiet month is not a clean month. Attention arrives when the river comes into the city, and leaves when it goes back inside its banks. The reading stays where it is either way. The current month is incomplete and is excluded from every figure here.

Where it comes from

Where it comes from

Not factories. The city.

Every figure in this band comes from one paragraph of one parliamentary answer, and every derived figure shows its own arithmetic. Nothing is inferred beyond the subtraction shown.

Sewage Delhi produces 3,596MLD
Treatment capacity built 3,474MLD
Capacity actually used 2,955MLD
Treated to the standard 2,014MLD

519MLD

of treatment capacity built and not used

3474 installed - 2955 utilised. At the same time, 641 MLD of sewage goes into the river untreated. The idle capacity is 81% of the untreated flow. Delhi is not only short of plants. It is not using the plants it has.

14 of 37

sewage plants that fail Delhi’s own discharge standard

37.8% of the city’s working plants do not meet the limits set by Delhi’s own pollution control committee. A plant that runs and does not clean is counted as capacity.

941MLD

of sewage that IS treated, and still fails the standard

2,955 treated minus 2,014 compliant. Nearly a third of everything Delhi treats does not meet the standard it is treated to. This is the figure that sits between the two numbers usually quoted — it is neither the 641 MLD that never reaches a plant, nor the capacity that does. It is water that went through the process and came out failing.

And it is not industry. CPCB inspected 189 grossly polluting industries on the Yamuna’s main stem in 2024. Their total discharge was 1.3 MLD — against 641 MLD of untreated sewage. On the government’s own numbers, industry is roughly one five-hundredth of the problem in this river. That is worth saying plainly, because it is not what most coverage implies.

Of those 189, 158 were operating and 49 of those — 31% — were breaking their discharge conditions or had no valid consent. 40 received a show-cause notice and 9 were ordered shut.

Two sources, published as two

This page’s readings are CPCB’s. A second compiler, RiverWatch India, publishes a CPCB-derived station table with something CPCB’s own PDF lacks — coordinates. It is used for placement and never for a value, and its disagreements are printed rather than resolved.

NAMAMI GANGE MISSION PHASE-II, 29 January 2026.

One division this page will not do. The release gives the cost of all 218 SANCTIONED projects and the capacity of the 138 COMPLETED ones. Those two figures do not divide into a meaningful cost per MLD, because the numerator and denominator describe different sets. Delhi's cost per MLD is computable only because its Yamuna reply gives cost and capacity for the same nine completed projects. The national figure is therefore not published here, and this note exists so nobody computes it later by mistake.

The page states the money and the reading side by side and draws no line between them. Completed is not the same word as working, and this page will not use one to mean the other. What is on the record is that the capacity was built, the projects were signed off, and the oxygen at four Delhi stations still reads below the detection limit.

The total ever spent on the Yamuna is not on this page. Figures of six and a half thousand crore and eight thousand crore circulate widely, sourced to a parliamentary panel through newspapers rather than to the panel's own report. Under this site's rules a figure reported by a newspaper is reporting, not data — it belongs in the coverage list below, never in this band. Closing this properly needs the panel report itself, and that is the largest single piece of work still outstanding on this page.

And why it is not a reply

All of that is people, and the river’s problem is 1,582 MLD of sewage a day that reaches it without being treated to the standard. Clearing a bank does not put oxygen back in water. Dissolved oxygen at 0.3 mg/L against a legal floor of 5.0 is not a measure of how many turned up; it is a measure of what Delhi builds and then runs.
