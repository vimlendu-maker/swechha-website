What that area is, in things you know

34,562 square kilometres is a little over twenty-three times the area of Delhi, burnt in a single season between November and June. It is not a satellite alert count. It is an area, mapped afterwards from Landsat imagery by India’s own Forest Survey.

And it is measured against a country where 28.0% of the forest and scrub is already classed as highly, very highly or extremely fire prone — 2,12,290.13 square kilometres of it.

This page will not tell you a fire count. It will tell you an area burnt, and separately how many hot pixels three different satellites saw. Those are three different numbers and none of them is “the number of forest fires in India”, because nobody publishes that.

What a fire even is

What a fire even is

Three satellites looked at the same five days over the same country and returned three different numbers. Almost every fire figure in circulation depends on which one was asked, and almost none of them says.

The same box, the same 5 days to 2026-08-21, three instruments:

MODIS1 km pixel · Terra / Aqua 121
VIIRS S-NPP375 m pixel · Suomi NPP 734
VIIRS NOAA-20375 m pixel · NOAA-20 694

DO NOT SUM THESE ROWS. Three sensors, the same five days, the same box, three different counts. Published separately and never summed: S-NPP and NOAA-20 see the same fires from different orbits, and MODIS at 1 km mostly does not see a small fire at all.

The season is shut. A low count here is the calendar, not an absence of risk — and it is exactly the condition under which a failed request and a true zero look identical. That is why the header of every response is validated and a failure is stored as null.

What FIRMS actually measures

A pixel hot enough to register in the infrared. Over India in March that is forest canopy, wheat and sugarcane residue, brick kilns, gas flares and rubbish fires — and the satellite cannot tell them apart.

One fire can produce several detections on one pass and none on the next. A fire the size of a football pitch is invisible to a 1 km MODIS pixel and obvious to a 375 m VIIRS one. So a detection count is a measure of what was visible to that instrument on those overpasses, and nothing more.

This page therefore never calls a detection a fire. This is NOT a count of forest fires, and not a count of fires. It is a count of detections: pixels hot enough to register. One fire can produce several detections on one pass and none on the next, and a detection can be a field, a kiln or a rubbish heap.

And this is why it leads the page

A detection is an event in a pixel. A burnt area is an extent, mapped afterwards from Landsat using differenced burn ratios. This is the forest-specific measurement that a FIRMS detection count is not, and it is made by the agency with the legal mandate.

So the hero figure on this page is an area from India’s own Forest Survey, not a count from a satellite feed. The detections are kept for the one thing they are uniquely good at: showing how unstable any single fire number is.

The same ten days of March, every year from 2013 to 2026, on one satellite. Comparable by construction, and a sample rather than a season.

Detections in 21-30 March, VIIRS S-NPP (science quality). Peak 1,78,058 in 2021, floor 65,208 in 2024 — a spread of 2.7× between two years, on the same instrument in the same ten days.

And across the whole period it is close to flat. 2013–2020 averaged 1,12,001 detections in the window; 2021–2026 averaged 1,04,962. The year-to-year swing is far larger than any drift between the halves, which is what you would expect from something driven by how dry a particular March happened to be.

A fixed window is a SAMPLE, not a season total, and it can miss a peak. It is comparable across years precisely because it does not chase the peak. And the forest itself

And the forest itself

Fire is one way a forest disappears. This is the measurement of the disappearing, and it has roughly doubled.

2.43M ha

of tree cover lost, 2001–2025

24,257 km², measured from Landsat at 30% canopy density. That is roughly 3.4% of India’s recorded forest cover, over twenty-five years.

93.7%

of that loss was not in a planted forest

The share of measured tree-cover loss that was NOT inside a planted forest. It is the answer to the commonest objection to this series — that it merely counts plantation harvests, which grow back. On this measurement the objection does not hold: nearly all of it is outside plantations.

Tree cover lost per year, 2001–2025

It has roughly doubled. 2001–2013 lost an average of 65,162 hectares a year. 2014–2025 lost 1,31,545 — 2.0× as much. The worst single year is 2017, at 1,79,260 hectares.

Of the whole total, 15.3% was primary forest — 0.37 million hectares of loss inside forest mapped as regional primary forest in 2001 — mature natural forest, the part that does not come back on a human timescale.

And India’s own report says forest cover went up. Over its last two assessments the Forest Survey of India recorded a net national increase of 156.4 km². Both figures are official, both are honest, and they measure different things. That argument has its own page.

Forest loss, in full

Tree cover loss is not deforestation and this page does not call it that. Tree cover loss is not deforestation. It counts canopy disappearing for any reason — felling, fire, storm, disease, harvest — and it does not say why.

Nothing here attributes tree cover loss to fire. Loss is counted for any cause and the dataset does not say why. Separating fire-driven loss from felling, storm and harvest needs an attribution layer this build does not have — so the two measurements sit side by side and no arrow is drawn between them.
